I'm sure you've met PCA before. If it were a food, it would be a croissant: everybody loves it, and the ingredients are simple (flour, butter, water), but most people have no idea how they turn into all those perfect layers. PCA was developed by statisticians, but under the hood it's linear algebra (hello, matrices!), and nowadays it's heavily used in machine learning as well.

I remember working on a project as an undergraduate that used PCA a lot. I could interpret the basic results, but nothing too complex because I didn't actually understand how we got there. One thing I struggled with a lot was the foundation of it all: linear algebra. So yes, I had to go back to it.

## Step 1: Vectors and matrices

Mathematicians and physicists, forgive me, but I find the computer science description of a vector the most intuitive: a vector is nothing more than a list of numbers. When we think about it as a location in space, each number tells us how far to go along one axis, and together they give the vector a direction and a length.

Two vectors are special here: **î = [1, 0]** and **ĵ = [0, 1]**. They have length 1 and point along the x and y axes, so they work as the "rulers" of our coordinate system. Every other vector is just a recipe of them: the vector [3, 3] means 3 steps of î plus 3 steps of ĵ.

![The vector [3, 3] built from 3 steps of î and 3 steps of ĵ]({{ '/images/blog/vector_3_3_ihat_jhat.png' | relative_url }})

When PCA creates new axes, each new axis gets its own ruler: a vector of length 1 pointing along it. Written in our original coordinates, these new rulers have different values, like [0.89, 0.45]. And those values tell us how much of each original axis (each gene) goes into the new one. Keep this in mind: it will come back when we talk about loadings.

## Step 2: What is PCA, and why use it?

PCA is a method that takes high-dimensional data and summarizes it in a new set of axes. These axes are built to capture as much of the variance as possible, so that a few of them are enough to represent the data.

In other words, PCA doesn't change your data. It just builds a new coordinate system to look at it from a better angle. The samples stay in the same positions relative to each other: we only describe them with new axes (the PCs) instead of the genes.

But why would you want to use PCA in your analysis? The most common reason: to see how similar or different your samples are.

Let's say you are doing RNA-seq and you want to compare the expression profiles of your samples. The human genome has around 20,000 protein-coding genes, so you end up with a big table of read counts per gene per sample: genes are rows and samples are columns.

If we had only two genes, A and B, we could plot the samples on two axes (gene A and gene B). But as soon as we add more genes, it becomes impossible to picture. We can't visualize more than three dimensions (and in my opinion, 3D is already a bit confusing).

This is where PCA comes in: instead of 20,000 gene axes, it gives us a handful of new axes that still show the main differences between samples.

These axes are called principal components, and they are ranked. PC1 captures the largest share of the variance in the data, PC2 captures the largest share of what is left, and so on.

(Remember: variance just means how spread out the data is from the mean, aka, how much it varies from the mean.)

## Step 3: How the principal components are created

Let's walk through a small example, inspired by StatQuest's PCA video. We measured two genes in six samples:

| Sample | Gene 1 | Gene 2 |
|---|---|---|
| S1 | 2 | 2 |
| S2 | 3 | 4 |
| S3 | 4 | 6 |
| S4 | 8 | 4 |
| S5 | 9 | 8 |
| S6 | 10 | 6 |

Each sample is a point in the plot below:

![Six samples plotted by the expression of two genes]({{ '/images/blog/pca_example_samples.png' | relative_url }})

**First, we center the data.** We calculate the mean expression of each gene (Gene 1 = 6, Gene 2 = 5) and subtract it from every expression value of that gene. This moves the center of the data cloud to the origin, without changing how the points are positioned relative to each other.

![The samples after centering]({{ '/images/blog/pca_centered.png' | relative_url }})

**Now we have to find the line that best summarizes the data.** We draw a line through the origin and project each point onto it. Ideally, we want the projected points to be as close as possible to the real ones, so we want to minimize the error: the distance between each real point and its projection.

For every point, this creates a right triangle with three sides:

- **a**: the distance from the origin to the real point
- **b**: the distance from the real point to the line (the error)
- **c**: the distance from the origin to the projected point

![Projecting the samples onto a line, with the triangle a, b, c for sample S5]({{ '/images/blog/pca_projection_triangle.png' | relative_url }})

And this is where Pythagoras comes in (and you thought you'd never use him again? Here he is):

**a² = b² + c²**

**a** represents the distance between the origin and the real point, and that will never change, so it is a constant. So if **b** gets smaller, **c** has to get bigger. Minimizing the error is exactly the same as maximizing the distance from the origin to the projected points.

That is why PCA looks for the line with the largest sum of squared distances, SS(d): the sum of c² over all points.

**The best line.** To keep this short, I'm not going to go over how the best line is found. Let's say we found it: it goes 2 units along Gene 1 for every 1 unit along Gene 2. This line is PC1.

A vector pointing along this line is [2, 1]. How long is it? Pythagoras again: the vector is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with sides 2 and 1.

length = √(2² + 1²) = √5 ≈ 2.24

To measure distances along the line, we need a vector of length 1, so we divide each component by this length:

[2 / 2.24, 1 / 2.24] = [0.89, 0.45]

![Shrinking the vector [2, 1] to a vector of length 1]({{ '/images/blog/pca_unit_vector.png' | relative_url }})

**Projecting the samples.** Let's check with sample S5. After centering, it sits at [3, 3], so a² = 3² + 3² = 18. To project it onto the line, we multiply its values by the unit vector and add them up:

c = (3 × 0.89) + (3 × 0.45) = 4.02, so c² = 16.2

That leaves b² = 18 − 16.2 = 1.8, which is a small error.

Doing the same for all six samples: the a² add up to 80, the c² add up to 70 and the b² add up to 10. So 80 = 70 + 10, and the SS(d) of PC1 is 70.

**From SS(d) to variance.** Now remember the formula for the variance of a sample:

variance = Σ (xᵢ − mean)² / (n − 1)

Since we centered the data, the mean is 0, so the top of this formula is just the sum of the squared distances to the origin. That is exactly our SS(d). So to get the variance along PC1, all that is left is to divide by n − 1:

variance along PC1 = 70 / (6 − 1) = 14

And this is the famous eigenvalue: the variance of the data along the PC.

**How much does PC1 explain?** Out of the total spread in the data (80), PC1 captures 70 and misses only 10. That is 70 / 80 = 87.5% of the variance, the number you see next to "PC1" on the axis of a PCA plot.

**Finding PC2.** PC2 has to be perpendicular (orthogonal) to PC1. Since we only have two genes, there is just one possible line:

PC2 = [−0.45, 0.89]

![PC1 and PC2, the new axes]({{ '/images/blog/pca_new_axes.png' | relative_url }})

Remember the 10 that PC1 missed (80 − 70 = 10)? That is exactly the SS(d) of PC2. So its eigenvalue is 10 / 5 = 2, and it explains 10 / 80 = 12.5% of the variance.

And that's it: with two genes, we can only have two PCs. In general, the number of PCs is at most the smaller of two numbers: the number of features (genes) or the number of samples minus 1. In a real RNA-seq experiment with 20,000 genes and 12 samples, that means 11 PCs at most.

Notice that the total variance (16) is the same as the sum of the variances of the two original genes (11.6 + 4.4). PCA doesn't create or destroy variance. It only redistributes it, putting as much as possible on the first axis.

## Step 4: Giving names to what we did

We have already calculated everything that matters. Now we just need the official names, which are the ones that usually scare people:

- **Principal component (PC):** a new axis. It is a linear combination of the genes, where each gene gets a different weight depending on how much it contributes to that axis. PC1 is the axis that captures the most variance.
- **Eigenvector:** the vector of length 1 that points along a PC. For PC1, it is [0.89, 0.45].
- **Loadings:** the components that make the eigenvector. They are the recipe of the PC: Gene 1 has a loading of 0.89 and Gene 2 has a loading of 0.45, so Gene 1 contributes about twice as much to PC1.
- **Eigenvalue:** the variance of the data along a PC. For PC1, it is 14.
- **Score:** where a sample lands along a PC. The number c we calculated for S5 (4.02) is its score on PC1. Think of the scores as the new coordinates of your sample in this new system: S5 used to be at [3, 3] on the gene axes, and now it is at [4.02, 1.34] on the PC axes.

## Step 5: The PCA plot

If we calculate the scores of every sample on both PCs, we get:

| Sample | PC1 | PC2 |
|---|---|---|
| S1 | −4.92 | −0.89 |
| S2 | −3.13 | 0.45 |
| S3 | −1.34 | 1.79 |
| S4 | 1.34 | −1.79 |
| S5 | 4.02 | 1.34 |
| S6 | 4.02 | −0.89 |

Plot these numbers and you have the PCA plot:

![The PCA plot, PC1 (87.5%) vs PC2 (12.5%)]({{ '/images/blog/pca_final_plot.png' | relative_url }})

One last thing before we finish. Everything we did here has a name in linear algebra: a change of basis. We built a new coordinate system whose axes are the PCs, and rewrote each sample in it.
